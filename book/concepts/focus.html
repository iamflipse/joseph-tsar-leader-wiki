Problem 2: Focus

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 09:40–16:40

The idea

Focus is a superpower everyone has, but most people can't reach it under the "choppy waters" of technology and modern life. When Tsar was preparing for a world-championship speech competition, he emailed champion speakers and got coaching from a few. What linked their different styles was that each had an intimate relationship with focus. They could "dance with it" and use it on purpose.

His comparison is curling your toes one at a time. Most people can't, but with enough time and intention you can activate muscles you didn't know you had. Focus works the same way: there are levels to it, and training it can "10x" clarity and precision.

Internal vs external influence

Most speakers are easily swayed by their surroundings, especially by listeners' faces.

  • External influence comes through the senses: what you see and hear. Someone frowns or raises an eyebrow, you read it as disapproval, and you bend your message to fit.
  • Internal influence comes from within: how you feel about the content, and staying true to it no matter what.

The core skill is noticing the thought that an external cue plants ("that person disapproves") and choosing not to engage it, not turning it over or letting it run away with you. In short, better control of your thoughts.

Focus breaks every 2–3 seconds

Most people don't realise their focus breaks every two to three seconds. A new thought arrives, and they change the message to make room for it. That constant switching and self-monitoring is why ordinary professional speech sounds unclear and messy. A speaker who holds focus through the whole message has "tremendous presence".

Focus never goes away. It is buried under mental noise: "Stillness is always with you."

The signal: comfort with silence

Tsar can tell how far someone has trained focus by one signal: how comfortable they are with silence. A person who can pause for long stretches mid-speech and sit in "that nothingness" has usually done the work.

How to apply it

1. Blindfold drill (external focus). Talk with someone while blindfolded and wearing earplugs or earmuffs. Notice how much easier it is to think, not doubt yourself, and stay on topic when the external input is gone.

2. "Something you'd die for" (internal focus). Talk about something you deeply believe in. Notice that you stop caring about the room. That is what internal focus feels like.

3. The Focus Anchor (test and train).

  • Close your eyes and hold in mind an object behind you (his example is the rolls of colored paper behind him). Define it and keep it in mind. That is your anchor.
  • While holding it, answer a question. The answer can be boring; it doesn't have to be smart.
  • Do it while people show you angry or disapproving faces (in his workshop version, on video, booing).
  • You are juggling three opponents: the anchor, the content of your answer, and the hostile reactions. Don't let your attention leave the anchor for long.
  • He says people usually laugh at some point when they realise they can stay steady and aren't controlled by what is in front of them.

4. Practise pausing. Deliberately leave silences while you speak and get comfortable in them. That is the outward sign of trained focus.
