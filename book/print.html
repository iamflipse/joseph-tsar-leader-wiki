The Articulate Ascent

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 01:40–04:40

The idea

Tsar borrows the saying that money doesn't remove your problems, it just gives you higher-quality problems. Communication works the same way. As you grow, the problems you face change in kind, not just in number.

The Articulate Ascent is his name for a chart of how a person usually grows as a speaker over a lifetime:

  • Early on: you try to impress people, and there is a lot of ego behind your words.
  • Later in life: much of that ego falls away, and you start to value simple speaking and storytelling, the way older people do.

His point is that you will drift up this curve anyway with age. The training is meant to get you there sooner by working on the right things now.

Low-quality problems: the symptoms

He says there are about 20 "low-quality" communication problems. These are the complaints he hears when people tell him their communication feels "off". The ones he reads out:

  • I take too long to get to the point.
  • I lose my train of thought.
  • I use filler words.
  • I struggle to explain complex ideas simply.
  • I sound scattered when I speak.
  • I don't feel like myself.
  • I over-explain.
  • I can't turn raw thoughts into polished words.

The full list is on screen in the video. Later he adds rambling, lack of clarity, poor word choice and poor information retrieval (about 18:20), and links them to an unregulated body.

Most people recognise themselves in five to ten of these. Which ones depends a lot on personality.

High-quality problems: the causes

Behind all of these symptoms are three high-quality problems: intention, focus, and body & breath. Tsar says that if you spend about two weeks over the next month working on those three, most of the low-quality problems go away.

How to apply it

  1. Go through the symptom list and mark the 5–10 that sound like you.
  2. Don't treat each one as a separate fix. Filler words or rambling are signals, not the disease.
  3. Map your symptoms back to the three causes (see Speech Is a Sport for the dashboard-light analogy) and train those.

Speech Is a Sport

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 05:00–06:20

The idea

Tsar's company has a saying: speech is a sport. Treat communication as a kind of athleticism, not as a talent you have or lack.

  • If you are bad at football, either you haven't had the right coach or you haven't done enough repetitions.
  • The same goes for speaking: either the wrong coaching, or not enough practice of the right things.

Every sport has two layers:

  • Techniques and mechanics: the visible moves.
  • Foundations: the strength that makes the moves work. Being good at kicking with your toes doesn't matter if your legs have no muscle.

The high-quality problems are the leg muscles of communication. They affect everything else you do.

The dashboard-light analogy

Low-quality problems (filler words, rambling, losing your thread) are indicator lights blinking on your car's dashboard. They tell you something is wrong under the hood.

  • The usual fix is to put duct tape over the light: hide the symptom, for example by drilling yourself out of filler words.
  • The real fix is to refuel the car or check the engine. When the cause is dealt with, the light goes out by itself.

How to apply it

  • Plan reps, not just insights. Pick a drill (see Practice) and repeat it on a schedule, like training sessions.
  • When a symptom shows up, ask "which light is this, and what's under the hood?" instead of "how do I hide it?"
  • Get feedback from someone who can see your mechanics. In his framing, that is the "coach" half.

Quotes

"We have this saying in our company that speech is a sport." — [05:08]

"Low quality communication challenges are like the indicator lights on your communications car dashboard that are blinking." — [05:58]

The Three High-Quality Problems

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 03:50–04:50 (overview), developed through 06:20–22:50

The idea

When Tsar is on a call or running a workshop, he listens for three underlying problems. Each one has a plain-language version that people say about themselves:

#High-quality problemWhat it sounds like from the insidePage
1Setting your intention"I don't know how to speak from my heart."Intention
2Holding / managing focus"I don't know how to manage my mind while I speak."Focus
3Body and breath control"I don't know how to control my nervous system."Body & Breath

Put simply: heart, mind, body. Tsar says that if you give these about two weeks of focused work over the next month, most of the low-quality symptoms go away.

He expects pushback ("I thought this was about choosing the right words") and answers that there are "layers to the game". Word choice sits on top of these three.

How to apply it

  • Use the table as a diagnostic. When a talk goes badly, ask which layer failed: was the intention unclear, did your focus break, or was your body tense?
  • Train one layer at a time with its drill: the one-word intention, the focus anchor, and posture plus lion's breathing.

"Number one, setting your intention, which translates to, I don't know how to speak from my heart." — [04:12]

Problem 1: Intention

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 06:20–09:40

The idea

Everything we say carries some intention. Tsar calls it "the flavor behind the words." When the intention and the words don't match, listeners sense that something is off, even if the words on paper are fine.

His demonstration: someone asks "are you listening?" and he answers "yeah, of course, I'm listening" in a dismissive tone. Written down, the words say yes. The tone says no. That mismatch shows the real intention: "my heart is saying something different than what my mouth is saying." (He adds an aside that women are better than men at sensing this.)

Intention shapes everything downstream:

  • An intention to harm brings more aggressive, hurtful word choices.
  • An intention to inspire brings more emotion in the voice, more emphasis on key words, and more uplifting language.

So setting the intention before you open your mouth is a high-leverage move. It changes the flavor of the whole message.

How to apply it: the one-word intention

  1. Take 30 seconds before you speak.
  2. Pick one word that will be the place you speak from, for example inspire, love or encourage.
  3. Speak from that word. He says choosing encourage changes how he shows up: his voice changes and his word choice becomes tighter and more congruent with the word.

Widen the range: the emotion wheel

To get better at naming your intention (and at reading other people's), Tsar recommends expanding your emotional vocabulary with an emotion wheel:

  • A wheel built around a small set of core emotions (he says five), which get more specific toward the outer rim.
  • He says it is not his invention. He studies it and links one under the video.
  • Why it helps: more words give you more places you can speak from.

Not covered in the video: which wheel he uses. The chart is only shown on screen.

Problem 2: Focus

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 09:40–16:40

The idea

Focus is a superpower everyone has, but most people can't reach it under the "choppy waters" of technology and modern life. When Tsar was preparing for a world-championship speech competition, he emailed champion speakers and got coaching from a few. What linked their different styles was that each had an intimate relationship with focus. They could "dance with it" and use it on purpose.

His comparison is curling your toes one at a time. Most people can't, but with enough time and intention you can activate muscles you didn't know you had. Focus works the same way: there are levels to it, and training it can "10x" clarity and precision.

Internal vs external influence

Most speakers are easily swayed by their surroundings, especially by listeners' faces.

  • External influence comes through the senses: what you see and hear. Someone frowns or raises an eyebrow, you read it as disapproval, and you bend your message to fit.
  • Internal influence comes from within: how you feel about the content, and staying true to it no matter what.

The core skill is noticing the thought that an external cue plants ("that person disapproves") and choosing not to engage it, not turning it over or letting it run away with you. In short, better control of your thoughts.

Focus breaks every 2–3 seconds

Most people don't realise their focus breaks every two to three seconds. A new thought arrives, and they change the message to make room for it. That constant switching and self-monitoring is why ordinary professional speech sounds unclear and messy. A speaker who holds focus through the whole message has "tremendous presence".

Focus never goes away. It is buried under mental noise: "Stillness is always with you."

The signal: comfort with silence

Tsar can tell how far someone has trained focus by one signal: how comfortable they are with silence. A person who can pause for long stretches mid-speech and sit in "that nothingness" has usually done the work.

How to apply it

1. Blindfold drill (external focus). Talk with someone while blindfolded and wearing earplugs or earmuffs. Notice how much easier it is to think, not doubt yourself, and stay on topic when the external input is gone.

2. "Something you'd die for" (internal focus). Talk about something you deeply believe in. Notice that you stop caring about the room. That is what internal focus feels like.

3. The Focus Anchor (test and train).

  • Close your eyes and hold in mind an object behind you (his example is the rolls of colored paper behind him). Define it and keep it in mind. That is your anchor.
  • While holding it, answer a question. The answer can be boring; it doesn't have to be smart.
  • Do it while people show you angry or disapproving faces (in his workshop version, on video, booing).
  • You are juggling three opponents: the anchor, the content of your answer, and the hostile reactions. Don't let your attention leave the anchor for long.
  • He says people usually laugh at some point when they realise they can stay steady and aren't controlled by what is in front of them.

4. Practise pausing. Deliberately leave silences while you speak and get comfortable in them. That is the outward sign of trained focus.

Not covered in the video: the list of focus-anchor questions he says is "below this video".

"Most people don't realize that their focus is usually breaking every two to three seconds because a new thought enters their mind." — [15:45]

Problem 3: Body & Breath

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 17:20–22:55

The idea

High performers (executives, CEOs, founders, salespeople) often have a dysregulated nervous system. Constant task-switching and stressful decisions build tension and control into their habits. Those habits helped them succeed, but they don't produce clear, powerful speech.

A tense body and a full mind put the nervous system on high alert. The speech that comes out is harsh, scattered, not calm, and that is where the low-quality symptoms come from: rambling, lack of clarity, poor word choice, trouble recalling information. When people say "I can't get to the point", Tsar often finds the root cause is simply that they aren't calm.

His example: an entrepreneur who designs luxury experiences for very wealthy clients, takes a call the moment he gets out of bed, hadn't meditated in a year, and asked to speak like Tony Robbins. Tsar laughed.

Being calm all the time is unrealistic; that is why monks leave for monasteries. The aim is to know how to regulate your nervous system, working on two parts of the body. "If you solve the body, you end up improving the mind."

1. Shoulders and posture

  • When Tsar looks for tension, the first and most common place is the shoulders and upper back: rounded shoulders, a "chicken neck" (head pushed forward).
  • Your favourite on-stage communicators share one thing: very erect posture. Tension takes resources and energy away from the mind. Good posture helps your flow of thought and your access to your best information.
  • He recommends a 15–20 second routine to loosen the shoulders and set posture before you speak. He doesn't show the routine in this video.
  • "If you get your posture right, you've created an excellent bedrock."

2. Breath: "authority is oxygen"

  • Good communicators breathe at the beginning and end of a sentence, not in the middle.
  • His law: "authority is oxygen". Where you breathe decides how authoritative you sound.
  • Rambling is often a breathing problem. If you breathe mid-sentence, your lungs refill and you now have air and power to keep going, and continuing feels natural. So the sentence never ends.
  • Much of good communication is retraining your breathing.

Lion's breathing

He has tried many breathing exercises, thinks most are fairly helpful, and doesn't rank one above the others. His default is lion's breathing:

  1. Take two sharp inhales to fill the lungs.
  2. Exhale through the mouth as if breathing out through a straw.

He describes it as copying the "primal startup sequence" animals go through before a chase (he describes animals scraping the ground two or three times). It builds some adrenaline, steadies the nervous system, and gives you a firm base to push off from.

The payoff of posture plus breath: more comfort, a body that feels prepared, better access to your thoughts, and a regulated nervous system. That base removes many low-quality symptoms.

How to apply it

  • Before speaking: loosen your shoulders, stand tall, then do a round or two of lion's breathing.
  • While speaking: breathe at full stops. If you notice you are breathing mid-sentence, finish the sentence on the air you have, stop, and breathe.
  • If you ramble, check your breathing before you check your words.

Quotes

"Good communicators do not breathe mid-sentence. There's a law that we talk about in communication where we say authority is oxygen." — [21:00]

"Anyone who deals with rambling is often a mid-sentence breather." — [21:12]

The Two Spiritual Laws

Source: Joseph Tsar, roughly 23:00–24:50

The idea

Tsar ends with two "spiritual laws" of communication. He frames them as the two fundamental battles good communication comes down to. They sit underneath the mechanics and give them "deeper roots".

Law 1: The Law of Source (the source principle)

His wording, condensed: the strongest speech comes from giving. If you need the room to approve of you, you lose your footing. If you bring something worth giving (your time, attention and presence), your voice steadies. Be the source.

In plain words: speaking to give to people brings more satisfaction, more fluency, and more power and impact. Speaking to take (approval, a win, validation) weakens it.

Apply it: before you speak, ask "what am I bringing them?" and not "what do I need from them?" This fits naturally with the one-word intention.

Law 2: The Law of Release

Every speaker faces a battle between control and surrender.

  • Control grips the words before they leave, trying to engineer the perfect idea.
  • Surrender lets the thought come out alive.

The goal is to speak with a steady hand and an open palm: enough structure to be clear, and enough release to be alive.

As you learn more techniques (including the ones in this video), you will feel the pull between "I want to structure and frame everything and control the outcome" and "what if I just spoke, and that was enough?" He doesn't settle it. He says the balance is yours to find and explore.

Apply it: prepare your structure (steady hand), then once you start speaking, stop editing in your head and let the words come (open palm).

Quotes

"The strongest speech comes from a place of giving. When you need the room to approve you, you lose your ground." — [23:27]

"Control tries to grip the words before they leave. Surrender lets the thought come forth with life." — [24:03]

"So the goal is to speak with a steady hand and an open palm." — [24:13]

Pre-Speaking Routine

Assembled from the drills Joseph Tsar describes in the video. It is not his own downloadable routine, which sits behind an email opt-in. The order below is this wiki's; he doesn't give a sequence.

Before you speak (about 2 minutes)

StepTimeWhat to doFrom
1. Shoulders~20 sLoosen your shoulders and upper back. Undo the rounding and the "chicken neck", and stand tall.Body & Breath
2. Lion's breath~20 sTwo sharp inhales to fill the lungs, then exhale through the mouth as if through a straw. Repeat a few times.Body & Breath
3. One-word intention30 sChoose one word to speak from (encourage, inspire, love…). Use the emotion wheel if you need a more precise word.Intention
4. Be the source~10 sAsk "what am I giving this room?" Drop "what do I need from them?"Spiritual Laws
5. Steady hand, open palm~10 sCheck your structure once, then decide to let go of controlling the words.Spiritual Laws

While you speak

  • Breathe at sentence boundaries, not mid-sentence ("authority is oxygen"). If you notice a mid-sentence breath, land the sentence and stop.
  • Don't engage reaction-thoughts. A frown or raised eyebrow plants a thought; notice it and leave it alone. Stay with your content.
  • Allow silence. A long pause is a sign of focus, not failure.

Training drills (outside the moment)

Do these as "reps" during the week (in his framing, speech is a sport):

  1. Blindfold talk. Talk with someone while blindfolded and wearing earplugs or earmuffs. Notice how much easier it is to stay on topic without external input.
  2. "Something you'd die for." Talk about a deep belief and notice that the room stops mattering. Remember that feeling.
  3. Focus Anchor. Eyes closed, hold an object behind you in mind, and answer a question (a boring answer is fine) while someone pulls disapproving faces. Keep coming back to the anchor.
  4. Emotional vocabulary. Learn the emotion wheel from the center out, so you have more intentions to choose from.
  5. Symptom check. Mark your 5–10 low-quality symptoms and track whether they fade as you train the three foundations.

Tsar's own timeline: about two weeks of focused work over the next month on the three foundations should clear most low-quality problems.
